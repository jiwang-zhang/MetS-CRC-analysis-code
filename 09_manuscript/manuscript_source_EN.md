# Metabolic syndrome and colorectal cancer share GPAT3 and PLAU as candidate mediators: Mendelian randomisation, machine learning, single-cell knockout and docking

## Abstract

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) has been associated with colorectal cancer (CRC), but whether the association is causal and which molecular nodes mediate it remain unresolved. We integrated bidirectional Mendelian randomisation (MR; MetS modelled as a latent genetic liability, with FinnGen CRC as the outcome), transcriptomic intersection of MetS and CRC differential expression, machine-learning prioritisation, immune deconvolution, single-cell virtual knockout and molecular docking in a fully in silico pipeline. MR supported a MetS-to-CRC effect (inverse-variance-weighted OR = 1.231, 95% CI 1.052–1.439, P = 0.0093), whereas the reverse direction (CRC to MetS) was not significant. The MetS and CRC transcriptomes shared 200 co-differentially expressed genes, 36 of which also lay within a TNF/NF-κB prior set derived from the MSigDB hallmark founder sets. Of seven hub genes retained by all three machine-learning algorithms, only GPAT3 and PLAU were concordant in direction with the CRC discovery analysis and remained significant in both GSE10950 and TCGA/GEPIA2. Virtual knockout of GPAT3 perturbed programmes of blood-vessel morphogenesis, extracellular-matrix organisation and myeloid activation. Immune deconvolution showed that PLAU correlated most strongly with tumour neutrophils (r = 0.376). Docking of the three candidate ligands against the PLAU catalytic domain predicted binding for all three, strongest for AGB (N-(1-adamantyl)-N′-(4-guanidinobenzyl)urea) and amiloride. These findings favour a causal MetS-to-CRC direction and nominate GPAT3 and PLAU as convergent nodes for experimental testing.

## Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) — central obesity, dyslipidaemia, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose, diagnosed when at least three of five components are abnormal [2] — carries a reproducible excess of CRC risk. Pooled prospective estimates give 1.13 (95% CI 1.12–1.15) across 31 cohorts [3] and 1.36 (95% CI 1.26–1.47) in a second meta-analysis in which risk rose with the number of components present [4]. The largest prospective test to date, 379,173 UK Biobank participants with 4,971 incident cases followed for a median of 11.8 years, retained a significant association after full adjustment only in some strata — younger men (HR 1.31) and older adults (HR ≈ 1.18), not younger women [5]. In 335,544 UK Biobank participants, systemic inflammatory markers carried part of the association [6], and within metabolically defined populations adiposity and body-shape indices move with CRC risk in the same direction [7]. In short, observational studies consistently show an association between MetS and CRC, but the mechanism remains unclear.

Two gaps follow. The first concerns causation. Observational designs, however large and carefully adjusted, cannot separate a direct effect of MetS from the shared causes that produce it, since age, adiposity, socioeconomic position and lifestyle act on both conditions, nor can they establish the direction in which any effect runs. The second concerns mechanism. What links MetS to colorectal cancer at the molecular level is still unknown [8]: the pathways proposed so far remain unproven, and the genes through which metabolic dysregulation would act have not been identified. With growing computing power and rapid algorithmic innovation, an increasing number of analyses can be carried out in silico, providing leads for wet-lab experiments and clinical practice. Researchers have already combined population data with multi-omics, network and machine-learning analyses to identify the shared mechanism linking MetS to prostate cancer [9].

We therefore integrated Mendelian randomisation, differential-expression analysis, machine learning, immune-cell deconvolution, single-cell analysis, virtual knockout, drug-set enrichment and molecular docking to investigate the causal relationship between MetS and CRC, the genes they share, the possible mechanistic pathways, and candidate therapeutic targets and drugs. We aimed to convert the observational MetS–CRC association into a testable causal and mechanistic chain, and to identify the shared MetS–CRC hubs and their potential as therapeutic targets.

## Methods

### Study design, reporting guidelines, and overall workflow

This study was conducted entirely *in silico* on publicly available data, as a four-layer computational pipeline. The causal layer tests direction, by bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR). The discovery layer nominates shared candidates by differential-expression intersection and functional annotation, then reduces them to a consensus set using three machine-learning algorithms. The characterisation layer examines the surviving candidates across cohorts and across resolutions — immune-cell deconvolution, single-cell reanalysis with virtual knockout, an independent transcriptomic cohort (GSE10950), and tumour-versus-normal expression in GEPIA2. The translational layer carries them into exploratory docking against a candidate target protein, as a first read on druggability. Every dataset, cohort and resource used at any stage of the workflow, together with its sample size and accession, is catalogued in Supplementary Table S1. The overall design is summarised in Fig. 1.

The MR component was conducted in accordance with the STROBE-MR statement [10] and rests on its three core assumptions — that the instruments are robustly associated with the exposure, independent of confounders, and act on the outcome only through the exposure.

### Mendelian randomisation

Bidirectional two-sample MR was performed with the TwoSampleMR R package (v0.7.6) [11] on locally downloaded summary statistics for both traits. MetS, the exposure, was instrumented with the common genetic factor GWAS of van Walree et al. [12], which models a latent liability built from the five continuous MetS components rather than the binary clinical diagnosis, in a European sample (effective n = 461,920). The outcome was colorectal cancer from the FinnGen DF13 release of C3_COLORECTAL [13], comprising 10,545 cases among 519,972 participants of Finnish ancestry. Instruments were the genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸); alleles were harmonised, and every instrument was required to clear the weak-instrument threshold F > 10. Linkage-disequilibrium clumping was applied with ieugwasr::ld_clump (clump_kb = 10,000, clump_r2 = 0.001) against the 1000 Genomes European reference panel: of the 6,718 exposure SNPs reaching P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, 193 independent variants were retained before outcome matching, and 185 survived harmonisation and were carried into the analysis. The European exposure GWAS and the Finnish outcome sample do not overlap, which satisfies the two-sample independence assumption. The design is illustrated in Fig. 2a.

The inverse-variance-weighted estimator was taken as the primary analysis [14], with MR-Egger [15], the weighted median [16], simple mode, weighted mode and leave-one-out analysis serving as sensitivity checks; heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q (I² = (Q − df)/Q), directional pleiotropy by the MR-Egger intercept, and outlier robustness by MR-PRESSO (v1.0) [17]. A reverse analysis (CRC → MetS), instrumented with CRC genome-wide significant SNPs under the same F > 10 threshold, served as a directional control, a non-significant reverse estimate being compatible with, though not proof of, the assumed direction. A multiplicative random-effects model was used throughout, so the reported intervals are the wider random-effects intervals. As an additional sensitivity analysis, instruments associated with the outcome at P < 0.05 were removed and the analysis repeated; because this filter uses outcome information and can induce collider bias, its result was reported only as a sensitivity check.

### Differential expression and co-DEG identification

Three Affymetrix GPL570 series — GSE21510 [18], GSE32323 [19] and GSE39582 [20] — were corrected for dataset-level batch effects with limma::removeBatchEffect and merged into a single CRC matrix of 748 samples (687 tumour, 61 normal) [21]. In this matrix, tumour-versus-normal differential expression was tested with limma's empirical-Bayes linear model under thresholds of |log₂FC| > 0.5 and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05. The MetS contrast used GSE98895 [22], a peripheral-blood mononuclear cell dataset profiled on the Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 array (GPL6947; n = 40, 20 MetS versus 20 controls), analysed with the same limma workflow under the same thresholds. To avoid cross-platform artefacts the two contrasts were analysed separately rather than merged into a common expression matrix, and co-differentially expressed genes (co-DEGs) were defined as the intersection of the two DEG lists, visualised as a Venn diagram.

Functional annotation of the co-DEGs was carried out with clusterProfiler [23] against Gene Ontology (biological process, cellular component and molecular function) and KEGG pathways (organism = "hsa"). The TNF/NF-κB prior set was assembled from the MSigDB hallmark collection (Broad Institute; release 2026.1.Hs) [24], in which each of the 50 non-redundant signatures is consolidated from multiple founder gene sets. The 132 founder gene sets listed on the MSigDB page for HALLMARK_TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/geneset/HALLMARK_TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB) were pooled, each gene was scored by the number of founder sets containing it, and the 1,803 genes present in two or more founder sets were retained. Intersecting this prior set with the 200 co-DEGs defined the 36-gene candidate set.

### Machine-learning hub prioritisation

Three algorithms were applied to the candidate set and their results combined by intersection. LASSO regression [25] selected features at the minimum λ of 10-fold cross-validation. Random forest [26] was grown with 500 trees at the default mtry (floor(√p) = 6 for the 36 candidate genes) to locate the error minimum, then refitted, retaining genes whose mean Gini decrease exceeded 2. SVM-RFE [27] eliminated features recursively down to the subset identified as optimal by 10-fold cross-validation (linear kernel, cost = 10, unscaled features). Genes retained by all three were taken as hub genes.

### Protein–protein interaction (PPI) visualisation

The hub genes were submitted to GeneMANIA [28] for *H. sapiens* under the default network weighting across co-expression, shared protein domains, physical interactions and genetic interactions, which places each hub in its wider functional and protein-family context.

### Immune infiltration analysis

The merged GEO tumour–normal transcriptome (748 samples: 687 tumour, 61 normal) was deconvoluted into 22 immune-cell fractions with CIBERSORT (LM22 signature, 1,000 permutations; relative mode, fractions summing to one per sample) [29], and the fractions were compared between tumour and normal tissue by Mann–Whitney U test. Within the 687 tumour samples, Spearman correlation was computed between each hub gene and each fraction and corrected for multiple testing by Benjamini–Hochberg, with nominal (P < 0.05) and FDR-adjusted results both reported.

### External validation

The hubs were taken to the independent microarray cohort GSE10950 (24 tumour versus 24 normal), where differential expression was re-tested by Welch t tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the hub genes, and where a logistic model built on the hub genes and trained on the merged GEO matrix was evaluated by ROC analysis. The training set was strongly imbalanced (687 tumour versus 61 normal), and no class weighting, resampling or cross-validation was applied, so the training AUC should be interpreted with caution. Confidence intervals for the AUC were obtained by the DeLong method. Hub expression was further examined in GEPIA2 [30], which contrasts TCGA COAD (n = 275) and READ (n = 92) tumours against GTEx normal colon (n = 349) and rectum (n = 318) [31,32,33].

### Single-cell reanalysis and in silico knockout

GSE132465 [34], comprising 33 samples from 23 patients (23 tumour, 10 normal, ten paired), was reanalysed with Seurat [35]. Of the 63,689 profiled cells, 54,541 passed quality control (at least 100 genes expressed and a mitochondrial fraction below 10%) and were integrated and reduced on 1,500 variance-stabilising (vst) highly variable genes. Clusters were annotated into six types (B, epithelial, mast, myeloid, stromal and T) and visualised by t-SNE, after which hub-gene expression was compared between tumour and normal cells.

Virtual knockout was then run with scTenifoldKnk [36], which implements the scTenifoldNet framework [37], restricted to cells from tumour samples across all six annotated lineages (no compartment stratification applied). Input was a variance-stabilising feature matrix of 5,000 genes with the target gene retained; of 40,042 cells from tumour samples, 18,133 passed the tool's internal quality control and entered network construction (nc_nNet = 5, nc_nCells = 300, qc_mtThreshold = 0.1, qc_minLSize = 500, nCores = 1, seed = 12345). Genes reaching adjusted P < 0.05 by Z-score constituted the knockout footprint and were submitted to enrichR for Gene Ontology Biological Process enrichment.

### Druggability screening and molecular docking

Candidate ligands were generated by querying the two cross-platform concordant hubs (GPAT3 and PLAU) through the enrichment interface implemented in enrichR [38] against the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) [39], a collection of drug- and compound-induced gene-expression signatures assembled from public compound–gene and compound–target resources and from published differential-expression experiments. Because the database indexes gene symbols, a hub indexed under an alias was queried under that alias rather than its primary symbol — GPAT3, for instance, under its alias AGPAT9. Enrichment was computed as an over-representation test (Fisher's exact test) of the input list against each drug signature. Candidates were ranked by the platform's combined score — the product of the logarithm of the enrichment P value and the z-score of the deviation from the expected rank — with signatures meeting the platform's false-discovery threshold retained for inspection. Ligands were carried forward when they were retained at that threshold, had a defined three-dimensional structure available, and represented one of two classes: a compound annotated against the docking target, and an approved drug with an experimentally reported inhibition constant, the latter allowing a predicted score to be set against a measured value.

Docking was then performed against the catalytic domain of the serine-protease hub, retrieved from the Protein Data Bank [40] with all chains other than the protease chain removed, using ligand three-dimensional conformers obtained from PubChem [41]. Refined blind docking was run on the CB-Dock2 server [42], which couples curvature-based cavity detection with AutoDock Vina docking and homologous-template fitting; no binding site was specified, so the search was not biased toward any known pocket. Poses were ranked by predicted binding energy (kcal/mol), and the best-scoring pose of each ligand, obtained in a single run, was retained together with its complex diagram.

### Software and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (v4.6.1). MR used TwoSampleMR (v0.7.6), with ieugwasr (v1.1.0) for linkage-disequilibrium clumping and MR-PRESSO (v1.0); LASSO used glmnet (v5.0), random forest used randomForest (v4.7-1.2) and SVM-RFE used e1071 (v1.7-17); CIBERSORT was run from its R script (v1.03) with the LM22 signature and 1,000 permutations; network visualisation used GeneMANIA and linkET, the latter not version-pinned and therefore reported without a version; single-cell reanalysis used Seurat (v5.5.0); virtual knockout used scTenifoldKnk (v1.0.3), which implements the scTenifoldNet framework [37]; enrichment and druggability screening used enrichR (v3.4); and docking used the CB-Dock2 web server. Random seeds were fixed at 12345 for random forest, SVM-RFE, the cross-validation procedures and the scTenifoldKnk run.

Unless stated otherwise, two-sided P < 0.05 was taken as the threshold of nominal significance, and multiple testing was controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg correction, with an FDR < 0.05 considered significant.

## Results

### Bidirectional Mendelian randomisation supports a MetS-to-CRC causal direction

To assess whether MetS causally influences CRC, we performed a two-sample MR analysis. MetS was instrumented with 185 genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), each above the weak-instrument threshold (F > 10; mean 54.8, range 29.8–689.7). Using the IVW method as the primary approach, MetS was associated with a 23.1% increased risk of FinnGen CRC (OR = 1.231, 95% CI 1.052–1.439, P = 0.0093; Fig. 2b–c). MR-Egger (OR = 1.095, 95% CI 0.711–1.686, P = 0.681), weighted median (OR = 1.126, 95% CI 0.899–1.410, P = 0.300), simple mode (OR = 1.107, 95% CI 0.619–1.978, P = 0.733) and weighted mode (OR = 1.198, 95% CI 0.482–2.978, P = 0.698) were non-significant but directionally consistent with the IVW estimate. Cochran's Q indicated heterogeneity across instruments (Q = 266.81, df = 184, P = 6.33 × 10⁻⁵; I² = 31%) and the MR-PRESSO global test flagged residual pleiotropy (RSSobs = 269.52, P < 0.001). The MR-Egger intercept gave no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (0.0022, SE 0.0039, P = 0.570). Removing the 13 instruments associated with the outcome in FinnGen (P < 0.05) left 172 SNPs and an essentially unchanged estimate (OR = 1.208, 95% CI 1.057–1.382, P = 0.0056), with MR-PRESSO now null (RSSobs = 136.09, P = 0.985) and heterogeneity likewise resolved (Cochran's Q = 134.53, df = 171, P = 0.982). Leave-one-out diagnostics (Supplementary Fig. S1) and the funnel plot of all 185 instruments (Fig. 2d) both indicated stability.

The reverse analysis, instrumented with seven genome-wide significant CRC SNPs (F range 35.8–93.4, mean 59.0), returned a non-significant estimate (IVW exp(β) = 0.970, 95% CI 0.938–1.003, P = 0.073; Fig. 2b); its diagnostics were Cochran Q P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ and MR-PRESSO global P = 0.001.

### Co-DEGs and the 36-gene candidate set

Having established the direction, we next asked which molecular nodes might carry it. In the merged GPL570 cohort (GSE21510, GSE32323, GSE39582; 748 batch-corrected samples), 4,319 genes were differentially expressed between tumour and normal tissue at |log₂FC| > 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.05, 2,369 upregulated and 1,950 downregulated (Fig. 3a). The same thresholds applied to the MetS peripheral-blood dataset GSE98895 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0, GPL6947; n = 40, 20 MetS versus 20 controls) gave 830 DEGs, 408 up and 422 down (Fig. 3b). The two lists overlapped in 200 co-differentially expressed genes, of which 93 were directionally concordant and 107 discordant. Per-cohort log₂FC and adjusted P values for every co-DEG, and hence the direction of each gene in the MetS contrast, are given in Supplementary Table S2 and underpin every direction statement that follows. Enrichment of the co-DEGs returned canonical NF-κB, TNF, IL-17, apoptosis and adipocytokine terms (Fig. 3c–d).

Given that GO and KEGG enrichment both converged on TNF/NF-κB signalling, we intersected the 200 co-DEGs with the TNF/NF-κB prior set assembled from the MSigDB hallmark founder sets (1,803 genes); the overlap comprised 36 genes (Fig. 3e) and defined the 36-gene candidate set (Supplementary Table S3a) taken forward into machine-learning prioritisation.

### Machine-learning consensus prioritises seven hub genes

The candidate set was submitted to the three algorithms under a fixed seed (12345): LASSO retained 12 genes, random forest retained 12 with GPAT3 ranked first in variable importance, and SVM-RFE reached its cross-validated optimum at 28 genes (accuracy 0.984) (Fig. 3f–h). Per-method ranked lists are given in Supplementary Table S3b–d. Seven genes survived all three selections — GPAT3, SGK1, TMCC3, PPARD, PLAU, LPCAT4 and TNFRSF21 (Fig. 3i) — and these hubs were used in every downstream analysis. Five of the seven were dysregulated in the same direction in the MetS peripheral-blood contrast and in the CRC tissue contrast, whereas LPCAT4 and PLAU moved in opposite directions. Table 1 reports the per-contrast effect sizes and adjusted P values for all seven.

### PPI network analysis

GeneMANIA placed each of the seven hubs in an extended functional neighbourhood of 20 related genes (27 nodes in total; Supplementary Fig. S2), dominated by co-expression, shared protein domains and physical interactions. Most neighbours were glycerolipid-metabolising enzymes of the GPAT/AGPAT/LPCAT/LCLAT/LPGAT families, mirroring the lipid-remodelling roles of GPAT3 and LPCAT4; TNFRSF21 sat next to TNFRSF1A from the same receptor superfamily, and TMCC3 next to the other TMCC-family members. Under the default weighting, four hub–hub edges were recovered: one shared-protein-domain edge linking GPAT3 to LPCAT4 (PFAM weight 0.073) and three low-weight co-expression edges — PLAU–PPARD (0.021), TNFRSF21–PPARD (0.015) and PLAU–TNFRSF21 (0.010). These edges were too sparse and too low-weight to coalesce the seven hubs into a single densely connected module: the hubs remained segregated into separate clusters.

### Immune infiltration links hubs to the inflammatory milieu

CIBERSORT deconvolution of the 748-sample cohort (61 normal, 687 tumour) into 22 immune fractions showed significant tumour-versus-normal differences for several subsets, among them plasma cells, CD8 T cells, follicular helper T cells, regulatory T cells, M0, M1 and M2 macrophages and mast cells (Mann–Whitney U; Fig. 4a). Correlating each hub with each fraction across the 687 tumour samples gave 154 tests, of which 77 were nominally significant and 65 survived Benjamini–Hochberg correction at FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 4b). The strongest associations were positive — PLAU with neutrophils (r = 0.376, P = 1.63 × 10⁻²⁴; BH-adjusted 2.51 × 10⁻²²), SGK1 with γδ T cells (r = 0.330, P = 5.87 × 10⁻¹⁹) and PLAU with M0 macrophages (r = 0.318, P = 1.21 × 10⁻¹⁷) — while the leading inverse pairs were SGK1 with regulatory T cells (r = −0.277) and LPCAT4 with activated CD4 memory T cells (r = −0.245). The correlations were computed within tumour tissue, and the 61 normal samples were too few for a parallel stratified analysis.

### External validation and diagnostic performance

In the independent Illumina cohort GSE10950 (24 CRC versus 24 normal), four of the seven hubs were significantly dysregulated, each moving in the direction seen in discovery: PLAU (+1.62, against +1.50 in discovery), GPAT3 (−2.28 against −2.76), SGK1 (−1.91 against −2.38) and TMCC3 (−1.52 against −2.19). TNFRSF21 (+0.05, 95% CI −0.28 to 0.37; BH q = 0.78), LPCAT4 (+0.15, 95% CI −0.32 to 0.63; BH q = 0.63) and PPARD (−0.10, 95% CI −0.41 to 0.22; BH q = 0.63) did not reach significance (Fig. 4c).

The GEPIA2 reference, which contrasts TCGA tumours (COAD n = 275; READ n = 92) with GTEx normal tissue (colon n = 349; rectum n = 318), placed PLAU and TNFRSF21 significantly higher and GPAT3 significantly lower in tumour (Fig. 4d–f). SGK1, TMCC3, PPARD and LPCAT4 did not meet the GEPIA2 default threshold (|log₂FC| > 1 with q < 0.01, from its one-way ANOVA of TCGA tumour versus GTEx normal) (boxplots in Supplementary Fig. S3). Intersecting the hubs significant in the independent GSE10950 cohort with those significant in GEPIA2 isolated GPAT3 and PLAU (Fig. 4g); both were also concordant in direction with the CRC discovery contrast (GPAT3 down and PLAU up in tumour).

A seven-gene logistic score trained on the merged GEO cohort reached an AUC of 0.976 in-sample (95% CI 0.944–1.000) in the imbalanced training cohort (687 tumour versus 61 normal, without resampling or cross-validation) and 0.943 (95% CI 0.881–1.000) on GSE10950 (Fig. 3j).

### Single-cell resolution of the cross-platform concordant hubs

Reanalysis of GSE132465 retained 54,541 cells across six major types (B cells, epithelial cells, mast cells, myeloid cells, stromal cells and T cells). The t-SNE embedding split by tumour and normal status (Fig. 5a) showed the epithelial and myeloid compartments expanded in tumour tissue and the mast, B and stromal compartments enriched in normal tissue (Fig. 5b; qualitative directions as plotted). GPAT3 was most highly expressed in myeloid cells and PLAU in stromal cells, both detectable in epithelial cells; GPAT3 was lower in tumour than in normal in every compartment measured in both conditions, whereas PLAU was higher in tumour than in normal when averaged across all cells (Fig. 5c); both single-cell directions were concordant with the bulk results.

Applying scTenifoldKnk to GPAT3 left 165 genes significantly perturbed (Fig. 5d), led by CRIP2, TMEM255B, NFIB and HYAL2 alongside a coherent endothelial and extracellular-matrix cluster (ROBO4, CDH5, MCAM, EMCN, PLVAP, COL4A2, ECSCR, RAMP2). Enrichment of the footprint was dominated by blood-vessel morphogenesis, vasculature development and extracellular-matrix organisation, with neutrophil and myeloid activation terms such as GO:0002283 also represented (Fig. 5e). In contrast, PLAU knockout returned no robust footprint, yielding only TYROBP (fold-change 2,818) and FCER1G (fold-change 2,158), magnitudes consistent with a numerical artefact.

### Drug prediction and molecular docking of PLAU

Druggability screening of the two cross-platform concordant hubs (GPAT3 and PLAU) against DSigDB returned 173 significant drug signatures, 172 of which involved PLAU (all FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Table S4). Three of these signatures — AGB (N-(1-adamantyl)-N′-(4-guanidinobenzyl)urea), amiloride and benzamidine — correspond to compounds with reported uPA-inhibitory activity and were carried into docking on that basis; their nominal ranks are not reported, because with a single-gene query the leading signatures share identical P values, z scores and combined scores, so ordering within that block is arbitrary (Supplementary Table S4).

The three ligands were docked against the catalytic domain of PLAU (urokinase-type plasminogen activator, uPA; PDB 5ZA9, 1.62 Å) [43] by CB-Dock2 blind docking with the AutoDock Vina scoring function (Fig. 6; Table 2). All three were predicted to bind in or adjacent to the S1 specificity pocket lined by Asp189, Ser190, Gly193, the Cys191–Cys220 disulphide and Ser195. The strongest predicted binding was for AGB (CID 4286), a synthetic uPA-inhibitor chemotype, at −8.2 kcal/mol. Amiloride (CID 16231), an FDA-approved diuretic with reported uPA inhibitory activity (Ki ≈ 7 μM [44]), was predicted at −7.3 kcal/mol in this single scoring run; the receptor had been co-crystallized with a hexamethylene-amiloride derivative. Benzamidine (CID 2332), a classic competitive inhibitor of trypsin-family serine proteases, scored −5.2 kcal/mol.

## Discussion

MetS has been associated with colorectal cancer for nearly two decades, yet the supporting evidence is almost entirely observational and cannot establish either causality or the molecular nodes that would carry it. We addressed both questions in one in silico pipeline: bidirectional Mendelian randomisation to orient the association, transcriptomic intersection and machine-learning consensus to nominate shared genes, cross-platform validation and single-cell perturbation to characterise the survivors, and exploratory docking as a first read on tractability. These layers are sequential rather than parallel: each takes the output of the preceding one as its input, narrowing the candidate set stepwise towards the two genes carried into mechanistic analysis. Compared with the prostate-cancer GPRC5B study that paired multi-omics with functional validation [9], ours substitutes formal causal inference at population level and enforces cross-platform concordance; compared with our group's earlier Mendelian-randomisation analysis in colorectal cancer that nominated TIMP1 [45], the present design takes MetS as the explicit exposure. To our knowledge this combination has not previously been reported for MetS and CRC.

The Mendelian randomisation layer orients the association, and it agrees in direction with the observational literature. Zhan et al. combined a prospective meta-analysis with MR across eight gastrointestinal cancers, but their MR component considered 17 MetS-related traits one by one — body mass index, waist-to-hip ratio, HbA1c and others — each as a separate exposure rather than as the clinical syndrome; for colorectal cancer only body mass index, waist-to-hip ratio and HbA1c were causal [3]. In the largest prospective cohort to date — 379,173 UK Biobank participants, 4,971 incident colorectal cancers, median follow-up 11.8 years — full adjustment left the association strongest in men under 60 (HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.15–1.50), smaller but still significant in older men and older women (HR 1.18; 95% CI 1.07–1.30 and 1.05–1.33) and absent in women under 60 (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.89–1.25), with raised waist circumference the only component consistently associated with risk [5]. Our instrument instead models the five continuous components as one liability [12], capturing the cluster as it is used at the bedside rather than any single trait, and it is not built from the lifestyle and socioeconomic variables that observational estimates must adjust for.

GPAT3 was downregulated in tumour in both microarray cohorts and in TCGA/GEPIA2. Single-cell analysis agrees with the bulk results: GPAT3 was lower in tumour than in normal cells in every compartment measured in both conditions (Fig. 5c) and highest in myeloid cells; the GPAT3 virtual knockout perturbed a programme dominated by vascular and extracellular-matrix genes, with neutrophil and myeloid activation terms also represented. These observations motivate a loss-of-function lipid-remodelling hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a tested model: perturbing glycerolipid synthesis may propagate into the stromal-vascular compartment. GPAT3 encodes an endoplasmic-reticulum acyltransferase catalysing the first committed step of de novo triacylglycerol synthesis [46] and is the predominant isoform in white adipose tissue, where deletion in mice removes most of the depot's GPAT activity and blunts diet-induced weight gain [47]; that metabolic role is what makes it a plausible node between MetS and CRC. In colorectal cancer itself the experimental evidence points the other way. Wang et al. found that GPAT3 promotes lipid-droplet accumulation and oxaliplatin resistance, suppresses immunogenic cell death and drives CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion [48]. The two need not conflict: their contrast is treatment-induced, between chemoresistant and chemosensitive cells, whereas ours is a baseline tumour-versus-normal comparison in untreated resection specimens, and they measure enzyme activity and lipid content where we measure transcript. A plausible reconciliation is that GPAT3 is dynamically regulated, lower at baseline in tumour tissue yet inducible under chemotherapeutic stress.

PLAU encodes urokinase, which drives pericellular proteolysis, matrix remodelling and invasion; PLAU has been identified among macrophage-related colorectal cancer biomarkers [49], and its receptor PLAUR sits in a FOXM1-driven axis implicated in colon cancer progression [50]. Upregulation across three platforms is therefore consistent with established biology. Unlike the other hubs, PLAU moved in opposite directions in the two contrasts — up in tumour tissue, down in MetS peripheral blood (Table 1); the two contrasts sample different compartments, tumour tissue and circulating mononuclear cells. On the MetS side the direction is the expected one: adipose tissue produces plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, the principal endogenous inhibitor of urokinase, so obesity is characterised by hypofibrinolysis [51], although PAI-1 acts on enzyme activity rather than on PLAU transcription and cannot by itself account for the difference at transcript level. Within the tumour compartment urokinase expression is instead driven by stromal and myeloid cells, consistent with the stromal localisation of PLAU (Fig. 5c) and the neutrophil and macrophage correlations recovered here. GPAT3 was directionally concordant between the MetS and CRC contrasts, whereas PLAU was concordant only across tumour platforms, so its link to MetS rests on shared-node membership and tissue-level evidence rather than on concordant change. A virtual knockout of PLAU produced no stable footprint, so this node rests on expression pattern and pocket engagement rather than on perturbation.

Druggability screening returned 173 DSigDB signatures, nearly all involving PLAU, and the three ligands carried forward — AGB, amiloride and benzamidine — were chosen because each has reported uPA-inhibitory activity. All three were predicted to bind in or adjacent to the S1 pocket, which in the crystal structure carries a 6-substituted hexamethylene-amiloride derivative [43]. A predicted binding energy is not an affinity, and still less a selectivity: amiloride is an approved diuretic whose uPA inhibition is micromolar [44], too weak and too unselective for repurposing on this evidence, and benzamidine is a non-selective serine-protease probe serving mainly as a control for pocket engagement. Each pose comes from one scoring run with no replicate and no reference structure for root-mean-square deviation, so an ordering spanning 3 kcal/mol carries little weight. What the layer adds is narrow: the pocket is one that a uPA-directed chemotype already occupies.

Several caveats should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the seven hubs are constrained as a set: they derive from a 36-gene set that already incorporates a TNF/NF-κB prior, and they did not coalesce into a module, only four hub–hub edges being recovered, the sole shared-domain one GPAT3–LPCAT4 (PFAM weight 0.073). The immune analysis corroborates that prior only weakly, with 65 of 154 hub–immune pairs remaining significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction, so the inflammatory attribution rests on annotation of the 200 co-DEGs, which were defined without a concordant-direction requirement and should not be read as a MetS-derived signature recapitulated in tumour. Second, no laboratory validation was performed, so every molecular finding is a computational prediction awaiting experimental confirmation. Third, the two contrasts are unbalanced: the MetS side rests on a single peripheral-blood dataset of 40 participants without replication, against 748 merged tumour and normal samples. Finally, the GPAT3 virtual knockout was conducted without random-gene or expression-matched negative controls; accordingly, the number of perturbed genes represents a nominal value rather than a reliable effect size.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that metabolic syndrome causally influences colorectal cancer risk and identifies a small set of shared transcriptional nodes as candidate mediators of that effect. Through the integration of bidirectional Mendelian randomisation, transcriptomic intersection, machine-learning prioritisation, immune deconvolution, single-cell virtual knockout and molecular docking, we found that: (1) MR supported a causal effect of genetically predicted metabolic syndrome on colorectal cancer risk; (2) the two diseases shared a set of co-differentially expressed genes, a subset of which lies within a TNF/NF-κB prior set; and (3) seven hub genes were recovered as consensus nodes, of which GPAT3 and PLAU take priority; (4) the GPAT3 virtual knockout perturbed a vascular and extracellular-matrix programme, and the three uPA-directed ligands were predicted to occupy the PLAU S1 pocket. These findings provide evidence for GPAT3 and PLAU as the prioritised candidates, with the seven-gene diagnostic model as a secondary candidate. They lay the foundation for protein-level validation and functional testing of both candidates in colorectal tissue stratified by MetS status. Testing these nodes in prospective cohorts would indicate whether they recur, and would support evaluating GPAT3 and PLAU as candidate intervention targets.
